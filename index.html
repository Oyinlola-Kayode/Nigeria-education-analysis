01 · Abstract
Project Summary

This project investigates Nigeria's education system using longitudinal data from the World Bank and UNESCO, spanning 65 years (1960–2025). The analysis follows the DIG Framework — Description, Introspection, and Goal Setting — to systematically profile, interrogate, and derive insights from a dataset of 9,722 records covering 449 education indicators.

Four stories emerge from the data: (1) a catastrophic 90% decline in government education spending over 50 years; (2) a persistently high out-of-school population of over 9 million children despite modest improvements; (3) a historic gender literacy reversal in which female youth literacy has overtaken male literacy for the first time in recorded history; and (4) a weak but discernible relationship between government investment and educational outcomes. The findings carry direct implications for policymakers, NGOs, and development finance institutions operating in Nigeria and across Sub-Saharan Africa.

This project was conducted as part of a structured data analysis learning programme, using AI as a collaborative analytical tool. All data is publicly available, and the full methodology is documented to allow complete replication.

03 · Methodology
Analytical Approach

This analysis follows the DIG Framework — a structured, three-stage approach to data analysis that ensures clarity of thinking before conclusions are drawn.

D — Stage 1

Description

Profile the dataset: dimensions, data types, time range, completeness, and column definitions. Goal: understand what you have before asking anything of it. Output: data dictionary, shape summary, quality report.

I — Stage 2

Introspection

Interrogate the data for patterns, anomalies, trends, and correlations. Ask: what is this data actually telling us? What is surprising? What is missing? Output: 4 identified story threads, visualisations, key statistics.

G — Stage 3

Goal Setting

Define a precise research question and analytical goal based on what introspection revealed. Output: master research question, 4-chapter story structure, findings, and policy recommendations.

AI Collaboration

AI-Assisted Analysis

Claude AI (Anthropic) was used as a collaborative analytical tool — for data profiling, pattern detection, visualisation, and structuring findings. All interpretations and conclusions were reviewed and validated by the analyst.

Analytical Steps

  1. Downloaded the World Bank Nigeria Education dataset from HDX (CSV format)
  2. Loaded and profiled the dataset: 9,722 rows, 6 columns, 449 indicators, zero missing values
  3. Identified and extracted 7 key indicators for focused analysis
  4. Conducted trend analysis across all key indicators (1960–2025)
  5. Computed gender gap statistics: male vs female youth literacy across 9 data points (1991–2024)
  6. Calculated female share of out-of-school children per year
  7. Benchmarked Nigeria's education spending against 6 comparator countries and UNESCO minimum
  8. Estimated annual education funding gap based on 2023 GDP of $477B and UNESCO 4% minimum
  9. Identified 4 story threads and structured findings into a coherent narrative
  10. Built interactive visualisations and packaged results into a replicable portfolio

01
The Spending Collapse
Nigeria's government has systematically defunded education over five decades — while its school-age population has tripled.

Story 1 — Key Findings

Nigeria's education spending fell from 3.21% of GDP in 1974 to 0.32% in 2023 — a 90% decline over 50 years. This is one of the steepest sustained education funding collapses recorded for any country in the World Bank database.
Every African comparator spends dramatically more proportionally: Kenya 5.3%, Ghana 4.0%, South Africa 6.2%. Nigeria's 0.32% is 10–19x lower than its regional peers and 13x below the UNESCO minimum recommendation of 4% of GDP.
The decline correlates with worsening classroom conditions: pupil-teacher ratios peaked at 46:1 in 2007 — 15% above UNESCO's recommended maximum. With fewer resources per child, learning quality deteriorates even where enrollment exists.
Despite the spending collapse, adult literacy has improved — from 55.5% in 1991 to 70.4% in 2024. This suggests that private schools, NGOs, and community-funded education are absorbing a substantial portion of the deficit left by government withdrawal.
To reach the UNESCO minimum of 4% GDP, Nigeria would need to inject an additional $17.6 billion per year into education — equivalent to the combined education budgets of several West African nations.

9 Million Children Left Behind
Nigeria has the largest out-of-school child population in the world. The data shows slow but real progress — and a persistent gender dimension.

Story 2 — Key Findings

Nigeria's out-of-school primary population grew from 7.5 million in 1999 to a peak of 10.3 million in 2010, before falling to 9.07 million in 2023. Progress is happening, but the trajectory is far too slow to meet SDG 4 targets.
Girls have disproportionately borne the burden of exclusion. Between 1999 and 2010, females consistently made up 55–60% of out-of-school children. By 2023, their share had fallen to 51.6% — still a majority, but a significantly improving one.
In absolute numbers (2023): 4.68 million girls vs 4.39 million boys are out of school. The 290,000 girl-majority represents a structural gender barrier in access — not a random distribution.
The decline from 10.3M to 9.07M between 2010 and 2023 represents 1.22 million children brought into education over 13 years — roughly 94,000 per year. At that rate, eliminating the out-of-school population entirely would take nearly 100 more years.

Story 3 — Key Findings

In 1991, male youth literacy (81.4%) was 18.9 percentage points higher than female youth literacy (62.5%). This was a significant structural gender gap rooted in historical barriers to girls' education — cultural norms, early marriage, economic deprioritisation.
By 2024, female youth literacy had risen to 81.43% — marginally overtaking male youth literacy at 81.31%. This is the first time in recorded Nigerian history that female youth literacy has matched or exceeded male literacy.
The journey was non-linear. Female literacy dipped to 51.6% in 2008 before recovering dramatically. This volatility may reflect data collection inconsistencies in conflict-affected northern states, or genuine disruption from the Boko Haram insurgency period.
The convergence coincides with major investments by UNICEF, USAID, and Nigerian state governments in girls' education programs, conditional cash transfer schemes, and community school initiatives — particularly in the North West and North East geopolitical zones.
04
Connecting the Dots
Do government spending decisions actually translate into literacy outcomes? The data suggests a weak but real relationship — with a crucial caveat.

Story 4 — Key Findings

The correlation between government education spending (% GDP) and adult literacy rate is positive but weak in this dataset, due to limited overlapping data points. This is consistent with broader development economics literature — spending is necessary but not sufficient for literacy improvement.
Adult literacy improved from 55.5% (1991) to 70.4% (2024) despite the spending collapse — strongly suggesting that non-government actors (private schools, faith-based institutions, NGOs) have compensated for the government's withdrawal from the education market.
This creates a dangerous two-tier system: children of wealthier families access private education of reasonable quality, while children of poorer families — disproportionately in rural and northern Nigeria — are left in underfunded, overcrowded public schools or out of school entirely.
The primary completion rate data (last available: 70.2% in 2010) combined with 9M out-of-school children suggests that a significant proportion of enrolled children do not complete primary education — pointing to retention, not just enrollment, as a critical challenge.
05 · Recommendations
Policy & Research Recommendations

Based on the findings, the following evidence-based recommendations are directed at policymakers, development finance institutions, and researchers.

01

Reverse the spending decline — urgently

Nigeria must establish a statutory floor of at least 4% of GDP for education spending, as recommended by UNESCO. The current 0.32% is incompatible with achieving any meaningful SDG 4 target. A phased increase to 2% by 2027 and 4% by 2030 is achievable with fiscal reallocation.

02

Target the 4.68M out-of-school girls specifically

Girls still constitute 51.6% of out-of-school children. Conditional cash transfers, community-based schooling, and free menstrual health provision in schools have demonstrated effectiveness in comparable contexts. These should be scaled nationally.

03

Fix the pupil-teacher ratio crisis

Nigeria's classrooms are dangerously overcrowded. A national teacher recruitment and retention programme, with particular focus on rural and northern states, is critical. Remote and underserved communities should receive salary incentives for qualified teachers.

04

Shift focus from enrollment to retention

With a primary completion rate of only 70% (2010, last available), a significant proportion of enrolled children are dropping out before finishing primary school. School feeding programmes, elimination of hidden fees, and flexible school calendars for agricultural communities are proven interventions.

05

Improve data collection frequency

Several key indicators (literacy rate, completion rate, out-of-school numbers) have large gaps between data points — sometimes 5–10 years. Annual or biennial data collection is essential for responsive policymaking and credible monitoring of SDG 4 progress.

06

Disaggregate data by state and zone

National averages mask extreme regional variation. The North West and North East zones have dramatically worse outcomes than Lagos or Abuja. Future analysis should use state-level data to identify where investment will have the highest marginal impact.

06 · Limitations
Limitations of This Analysis

Transparent reporting of limitations is essential for any credible data analysis. The following constraints should be considered when interpreting these findings.

No sub-national disaggregation

All data is at the national level. Nigeria's 36 states and 6 geopolitical zones have vastly different education outcomes. National averages can conceal extreme inequality. State-level analysis would require separate, harder-to-access datasets.

Sparse literacy data points

The literacy rate indicator has only 6 data points across 33 years (1991–2024). This makes trend analysis and correlation calculations statistically unreliable. Conclusions about literacy trends should be treated as indicative, not definitive.

Completion rate data is outdated

The most recent primary completion rate data available is from 2010. Any conclusions about current completion rates are extrapolations. This is a significant gap given 14 years of demographic and policy change since that data point.

Causality cannot be established

This is a descriptive and exploratory analysis. Correlations identified (e.g. between spending and literacy) do not imply causation. Establishing causal relationships would require controlled study designs or instrumental variable methods beyond this analysis.

Out-of-school data has temporal gaps

Out-of-school children data is available for only 10 years between 1999 and 2023, with an 11-year gap between 2010 and 2023. Trends during this gap period cannot be observed directly and may have included significant changes.

Informal education not captured

A substantial portion of Nigerian children receive education through Quranic schools (Almajiri), informal apprenticeships, and community-based programmes not captured in World Bank enrollment or literacy metrics. The true education landscape may be more complex than this dataset reflects.

07 · Replication Guide
How to Replicate This Analysis

This analysis is fully reproducible. Follow the steps below to replicate results from scratch, or run the included Python script to automate the process.

Step-by-step replication

  1. Visit https://data.humdata.org/dataset/world-bank-education-indicators-for-nigeria and download the CSV file
  2. Install Python 3.8+ and required libraries: pip install pandas matplotlib seaborn
  3. Save the CSV to a known path on your computer (e.g. ~/data/education_nga.csv)
  4. Run the Python script from command line: python analyze.py --file ~/data/education_nga.csv
  5. Output charts and a summary CSV will be saved to an output/ folder in the same directory
  6. Review the printed summary statistics in the terminal for the full data profile

Environment & Dependencies

Python 3.8+  |  pandas >= 1.3.0  |  matplotlib >= 3.4.0  |  seaborn >= 0.11.0  |  argparse (standard library)

All libraries are open source and freely available via pip. No API keys or paid services required.
